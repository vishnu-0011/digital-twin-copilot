Why this project exists

Machines warn before they break. Most factories miss it.

A worn spindle bearing doesn't fail out of nowhere. Its vibration creeps up for hours or days first. The signal is there. What's missing is someone, or something, turning it into a clear decision in time: which machine, what to fix, and by when.

The warning window
The core problem

The gap between a sensor reading and a decision

Plants usually choose between two bad options: fix things after they break, or service them on a calendar. Both ignore the condition of the actual machine. The data to do better often already exists, but it arrives as numbers nobody has time to interpret.

01

Breakdowns are found too late

A machine that fails mid-shift stops its line, and the repair is unplanned: diagnose, find parts, call people in. That takes far longer than a planned service, and a failure mid-cut can scrap the part on the table.

02

Calendars don't know the machine

Servicing every N hours replaces healthy parts on machines running light work, and still misses the one wearing fast on hard material. Wear depends on load, material and luck, not the date.

03

The data isn't a decision

"Vibration 3.1 mm/s, RUL 47 cycles" means nothing to a busy supervisor. Classic monitoring screens flood people with alarms, so warnings get muted. People need the answer, not the reading.

The hard part isn't predicting failure. It's getting the right person to act on it in time, with enough trust to stop a running machine.

Why an aerospace machine shop

Where the problem hurts most

We set the twin in a plant that machines jet-engine and airframe parts, because every part of the problem is sharper there.

Hard metals

Titanium and nickel alloys

Tough, heat-resistant materials wear tools and spindles quickly, so condition changes faster than a schedule assumes.

Valuable parts

Hours of work per part

Blades, blisks and bulkheads are cut from expensive billets over long cycles. A failure mid-cut can scrap all of it.

Few machines

Specialised bottlenecks

A 5-axis mill or vacuum furnace often has no spare. When it stops, everything downstream waits.

Strict process

Every fix is documented

Work follows written procedures and needs sign-off, so a useful copilot must cite the procedure and leave approval to a person.

Why we took on this project

The idea is proven. Using it is still hard.

Predictive maintenance isn't new. What's rare is a system that carries it all the way to an approved repair, explains itself, and can be tested honestly. That's the gap we wanted to close.

01 · Close the whole loop

Most demos stop at a chart

A dashboard that shows a risk score still leaves someone to decide what to do. We wanted the full chain: sense, detect, predict, decide, and fix, ending in a work order a supervisor can approve in one click.

02 · Earn trust

Show the uncertainty, keep a human in charge

Nobody stops a machine on a black box's word. Every prediction shows its error range, every fix cites its procedure, and nothing happens until a person approves it.

03 · Test the claim

A twin lets you check the promise

Instead of quoting savings, the twin can run the same plant forward twice, with and without the copilot, and count what changes. You can check the claim yourself.

04 · Make it understandable

Readable by the people who use it

Plain status words, deadlines in clock time, and a live 3D plant you can walk around. The same system should make sense to a supervisor, a technician and a manager.

What we built

One loop, from vibration to a signed-off repair

1

Sense

A digital twin of 8 machines streams a reading every cycle: vibration, temperature, power.

2

Detect

Unusual readings are flagged and graded against the ISO vibration zones.

Isolation Forest · ISO 10816-3
3

Predict

A neural network estimates how many cycles each machine has left, with an error range.

Dilated TCN · remaining useful life
4

Decide

Agents diagnose the likely cause, find the procedure, and set a deadline in clock time.

LangGraph agents · SOP search
5

Fix

A supervisor approves the work order; the machine is serviced and comes back healthy.

Honest limits

What this is, and what it isn't

  • The plant is simulated. The sensor stream has the same shape as real data, but no real machine has been connected yet.
  • The models are trained on the simulator, so their accuracy on a real spindle is unknown until tested on real data.
  • The copilot never acts alone. It drafts the work order; a person approves it.
  • The savings figures above are industry best cases, not a promise. The forecast shows the effect on this simulated plant only.
